01 — Resource cost

How light it actually is

The honest caveat on that 11 MB. The AirStat instance measured here was freshly launched and its panel was never opened. An earlier instance that had been used — panel opened, settings and the colour picker shown — sat steady at a 70 MB footprint and never gave it back: 41 MB dirty MALLOC_SMALL, plus CoreAnimation, CoreUI image data and IOAccelerator regions that only appear once the UI has drawn. So 11 MB is the honest cold number and ~70 MB is the honest warm one. Against a warm AirStat, iStat Menus' 64 MB suite is roughly a tie, not a 5.8× win.
Energy impact. Sampled separately over n=60: AirStat 0.04 mean, Stats 1.94 — a 48× gap that tracks the CPU result exactly. AirStat's DispatchSourceTimer is scheduled with leeway of 25% of the interval, which lets the kernel coalesce its wakeups with other timers; a 5-second profile caught the sampling queue on-CPU for 3 of 3,690 samples (0.08% of wall time), two of those inside a single SMC thermal read.

The CPU aggregate is the only figure that drifts, and it drifts in the defensible direction: across 20 paired samples AirStat read a mean 1.1 percentage points below top on a ~61% busy machine (range −4.1 to +1.0). The two never share an identical window, and top charges its own overhead into the figure it prints, so a small negative bias is expected rather than a bug.

What the accuracy discipline actually buys. The collector protocol forbids returning a fabricated or zeroed value to paper over a failed syscall, and forbids any rate computed from a zero elapsed time. That rule is enforced by tests, and it is the reason every number above closes. Concretely: the GPU collector primes and discards a read on start, because PerformanceStatistics reports utilisation since the last read by any process — without that, the first sample inherits an unknown window and was observed reporting 98% on a fully idle GPU. Neither competitor's published behaviour documents handling this.
03 — Architecture

Why it is fast

The performance result is not incidental; it comes from four decisions visible in the source.

  • Demand-driven sampling. SamplingActivity has five levels. A collector nothing is displaying is never sampled and releases its resources; the process collector — by far the most expensive — only runs when the panel is actually on screen. Screen asleep suspends the timer entirely.
  • No allocation on the tick path. Tick buffers are swapped rather than reassigned, MIBs are resolved once instead of walking sysctlbyname's name resolver per sample, and SMC request/reply buffers live for the collector's lifetime.
  • Charts drawn as shapes, not Canvas. SwiftUI's Canvas brings up the Metal renderer — a fixed ~93 MB process-global allocation that is never returned. Avoiding it is most of the memory gap.
  • Observation-driven redraw. The status item re-renders when the model changes, not on a timer. No snapshot, no redraw, no wakeup.
Where the competition spends it. Stats' ProcessReader shells out to /bin/ps on a 1-second interval per its source, which would be a plausible explanation for its 1.9% floor — but I could not confirm it on this machine: a 2,000-iteration tight loop caught zero child processes, so that module is evidently not enabled in the default install. The 40× CPU gap is measured; the mechanism behind it is not established. Stats also carries a universal binary (17 MB vs AirStat's 4.6 MB arm64-only) and has open upstream reports of 400–470 MB memory use, so 145 MB here is not an outlier for it.

The honest read: Stats is not the benchmark to beat — iStat Menus is. Stats is the app most people compare against because it is free and popular, and AirStat beats it by a wide, reproducible margin. But iStat Menus' reputation for efficiency is deserved: its menu bar process idles at 0.116% CPU. AirStat is still ahead, by 2.5× on CPU, and it does it in one process with no launch agents against iStat's three processes, two launch agents and 163 MB of installed footprint.

Where AirStat is behind on scope, not efficiency: iStat Menus ships weather, per-sensor customisation, notification thresholds with history, and far more menu bar widget configurations. AirStat covers 16 menu bar metrics and 9 panel modules, which is the core of what those apps do — but it has deliberately removed panel configurability, and it has no history persistence across launches.

05 — Defects

What the index turned up

The running instance restarted mid-benchmark, unexplained unresolved

PID 27697 disappeared at 21:31:38 and launchd started PID 34683 in its place. No crash report was written to ~/Library/Logs/DiagnosticReports, and nothing in the unified log named the process. I could not attribute it to my own tooling — I only ever read from it. It may have been a manual relaunch. Worth reproducing before shipping, because a silent restart of a login-item menu bar app is exactly the failure a user would never notice and never report.

Disk row does not add up for the reader real

The probe prints 356 GB / 494 GB (71.9%) free 179 GB. Those three numbers cannot be reconciled: 356 + 179 > 494. usedBytes is derived from the strict freeBytes (which matches df), while the displayed "free" is availableBytes, which adds purgeable space back. Both figures are individually correct and the trade-off is documented at length in DiskCollector.swift:180 — but the surface still shows a user two numbers that visibly contradict each other. Either label the purgeable component or derive used from the same figure.

Warm memory never comes back down measured

Once the panel and settings have been shown, the footprint settles at ~70 MB and stays there — 41 MB of it dirty MALLOC_SMALL. For an app whose entire pitch is being lighter than the competition, and which idles at 11 MB cold, that is the single highest-value thing left to investigate. Note the ColorPicker / NSColorPanel hazard already documented in CLAUDE.md is in this same path.

availableBytes doc comment is inverted doc bug

Snapshots.swift:336 documents it as "free space the OS will hand out without purging purgeable caches" — but the collector assigns free + purgeable, which is the opposite. The long comment in DiskCollector.swift has it right; this one contradicts it.

Adapter name and wattage disagree newly exercised

Probe reports 70W USB-C Power Adapter with adapterWatts = 60. PowerCollector.applyAdapter is documented as never having been exercised because no charger was ever connected during development. It is now, and it mostly works — but the two fields it publishes disagree, so one of them is being read from the wrong key.

Build artefacts committed to the repo root hygiene

~90 .o, .d and .swiftdeps files sit loose in the project root alongside Package.swift. Given CLAUDE.md's own warning that stale incremental state silently links old objects, these are worth gitignoring and clearing.

The thing that most distinguishes this code from its competitors is not the architecture — it is that the comments record measurements, not intentions. The memory collector explains why its headline runs 0.84 GiB below Activity Monitor's and what arithmetic reproduces Apple's figure instead. The power collector explains why systemWatts is deliberately left nil: the obvious SystemLoad key disagreed with V×A by up to 45% at the same instant, so publishing it would have contradicted the batteryWatts sitting beside it. The CPU collector documents that the kernel numbers logical CPUs from the last performance level backwards, and how that was verified with spinners. That is why the accuracy column above is clean.

Method — All three apps sampled by one top -l 2 -s 1 invocation per interval so windows are shared; n=160 per process for the three-way run, n=192 for the AirStat/Stats run, n=60 for energy, n=14 and n=20 for accuracy. An interim cut at n=58 gave the same figures to within 0.03 pp, so the ranking is stable. Machine was under a sustained artificial load throughout: six orphaned yes processes, started 30 Jul during this project's own CPU verification work, have been pegging six cores for two days (load average 7.6–8.0). All apps were affected equally, so the comparison holds, but absolute CPU percentages are measured against a busy machine, not an idle one. iStat Menus was benchmarked only after its trial setup was completed and its menu bar items enabled; before that it was inactive and its numbers were discarded. Stats and iStat Menus were installed via Homebrew for this comparison and remain installed.
